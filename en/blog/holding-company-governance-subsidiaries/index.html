Holding Company Governance: How to Run Your Subsidiaries with Clarity and Control

Why does a holding company need its own governance?

A holding company owns stakes in subsidiaries that each have their own board, management and activities. The more companies there are, the more questions arise: who approves the budget? Who appoints the managers? Which decisions go up to the parent? If these are not answered in writing, habit and personal judgement decide them, and that is where gaps begin.

Define what the holding decides and what it leaves to the subsidiary

  • Write an authority matrix that shows the decisions reserved for the holding (such as major investments, appointing senior officers and changing capital) and the ones each subsidiary decides itself.
  • Set clear financial limits for each approval level.
  • Review each subsidiary's articles of association so they match this matrix.

Subsidiary boards: who represents the holding?

The holding is represented on its subsidiaries' boards by members who understand the group's direction. What matters most is that each member knows they serve the interest of the company whose board they sit on, and discloses any conflict of interest. It also helps to split roles between the board and executive management in each company, as in any board of directors.

Group policies with measured flexibility

The holding sets framework policies for the group, such as procurement, delegation, conflicts of interest, risk and compliance, and lets each company detail them to suit its activity, documenting any exception. This avoids a conflicting policy in every company.

Regular reports and comparable indicators

  • Define one report template that subsidiaries send to the holding at a fixed frequency.
  • Make indicators comparable across companies: financial, operational, compliance and risk.
  • Track the decisions of subsidiary boards and their implementation status in one place.

Common mistakes

  • Running subsidiaries through messages and phone calls instead of a written matrix.
  • Mixing holding decisions with subsidiary decisions, so responsibility is lost.
  • Board meetings that are not documented in a consistent way.
  • No single register of companies, their board members and their authorities.

Conclusion

Group governance succeeds when it is clear who decides what and when reports arrive in one format. Start with a group authority matrix and a register of companies and their boards, then standardise reporting.

Note: legal and regulatory requirements vary by company type and listing status, so check the laws in force when applying.
